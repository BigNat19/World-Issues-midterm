Unit 1

Perspective, Bias & the Media

Big idea: everyone sees the world through a lens. Good thinkers know where their lens comes from, understand other people's, and question who is telling the story.

Where our perspectives come from

  • Socio-economic status (SES): a measure of a person's or group's position in a community, based on things like parents' occupations, income, education and neighbourhood.
  • Life experiences: gender, ethnicity, age, religion and other factors shape the experiences we have.
  • Perspectives can and do change as people live through new experiences or get older.
  • People aren't consistent "teams": someone can hold a left-wing view on one issue and a very different view on another (the political spectrum is issue by issue).

Why point of view matters

  • Better understanding: helps us understand the opinions, beliefs and values of others.
  • Conflict resolution: looking for differing views helps settle arguments.
  • Including others: asking which viewpoints are represented and which are missing makes our thinking more inclusive.

Stakeholders: the Downtown Guelph encampments example

A stakeholder is anyone affected by, or with an interest in, an issue. For downtown encampments the stakeholders included business owners, downtown citizens, people experiencing homelessness, and city council / the mayor. For each, ask: what's their rationale, how useful is their view, and where can you get more info on it?

Hard empathy, strawman & steelman

Easy empathy is understanding people who already think like you. Hard empathy is understanding a perspective you find frustrating without excusing or agreeing with it. You don't have to agree with someone to understand them.

StrawmanSteelman
Make the other side's argument weaker so it's easier to attack. It's what happens when we skip hard empathy.Explain the other side's argument in its strongest, fairest form, even if you disagree. It's hard empathy in action.
"They're just stupid." "They don't care about people." "They want to ruin the country.""I understand why someone might think that because…" "A fair concern they have is…" "The strongest version of their point is…"
Shuts down discussion, creates division, you learn nothing, you stay in your bubble.Builds hard empathy, shows maturity, helps you understand the real issue, and makes your own arguments better.
Steelmanning is empathy for someone's reasoning, not necessarily their conclusion. A good steelman also needs the best evidence.

Class practice topics: school uniforms; should Canada increase military spending?

Facts vs opinions

Facts are OBJECTIVEIndisputable, concrete truths that can be verified by accurate observation and measurement. Your personal experience can't change them.
Opinions are SUBJECTIVEBeliefs you hold about events. They develop when you consider the facts in light of your values, so they come from your perspective.

Being a critical news consumer: vertical vs lateral reading

Class activity: American Academy of Pediatrics vs American College of Pediatricians. The names sound almost identical, but they are very different organizations.

  • Vertical reading: judging a source by staying on its own website (scrolling, reading its "About" page, how professional it looks). This is easy to fool.
  • Lateral reading: leaving the site and opening other tabs to see what independent sources say about who's behind it. This is what professional fact-checkers do.
Don't judge a source by how it describes itself. Check what others say about it.

The danger of a single story (Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie)

  • A single story is when the same story is told over and over about a people or place we don't know first-hand (e.g., "Africa" = poverty, safari, war).
  • The danger: it creates stereotypes. "The problem with stereotypes is not that they are untrue, but that they are incomplete": half-truths, not the full truth.
  • Power is the ability not just to tell a story, but to make it the definitive story. This links to race, gender and class.
  • The fix: seek a balance of stories.

Historical examples of the single story:

  • John Locke (1561): a London merchant who sailed to West Africa and described Black Africans as "beasts who have no houses" and "people without heads, having their mouths and eyes in their breasts."
  • Rudyard Kipling: a prominent writer of the British Imperial era whose work portrayed the Empire as a civilizing force (the imperialist mindset).
  • Modern pop culture: Western films and media that keep repeating the same images of other regions.

VIP #1 · Edward Said: Orientalism

Palestinian-American intellectual, literary theorist and cultural critic (1935–2003).

  • His book Orientalism critically examines how Western scholarship and culture represented the Middle East, Asia and "the Orient."
  • He argued the West depicted the East as exotic, inferior and "the Other", which helped justify colonial and imperialist ideas.
  • He urges us to question the sources of knowledge and how power and ideology shape the narratives we receive.
Media and literature often show "the Other" in stereotyped, biased ways. These misrepresentations cause real-world harm, so Western-centric views need to be challenged.

Guiding question: How do media representations shape how we see people from other parts of the world?

VIP #2 · Marshall McLuhan: "The medium is the message"

  • The medium (how information is delivered) has a profound impact on how we perceive the message, often more than the content itself.
  • Media technologies aren't just channels. They shape human behaviour and society.
  • Shifts from one medium to another (print → TV → internet) have cumulative effects on society.
  • Famous quote: "We shape our tools and thereafter our tools shape us."

Questions to ask about any medium

  • What habits or behaviours does it encourage?
  • How does it change the way we access information?
  • Does it shape power dynamics (who controls the narrative)?

Social media as McLuhan's theory in action

  • Algorithm-driven feeds: personalize content based on behaviour, so the medium shapes our experience.
  • Notifications & the "like" button: dopamine hits that condition us to seek quick validation and build addictive habits.
  • Stories & short-form video: push brief, surface-level engagement over deep reflection, which shortens attention spans.

Art as a medium · Chris Hondros: a war photographer whose images show how photography can carry emotional impact that articles or documentaries may not. That's McLuhan's point: the form changes how we feel the story.

Information overload: an abundance of information means the truth often loses. See also The Social Dilemma and confirmation bias.

VIP #3 · Stuart Hall: Encoding & Decoding

  • Encoding: producers of content (news, TV, ads) embed meanings and values that reflect certain ideologies or viewpoints.
  • Decoding: the audience interprets the message. They are not passive: they decode based on their own experiences, social position and cultural background.
  • Cultural identity is a "lens" through which we view everything.
  • Media doesn't just reflect the world; it helps shape our ideas.

Three types of decoding

Dominant (preferred)Audience accepts the message exactly as the producer intended.
NegotiatedAudience partly accepts the message but modifies or resists parts of it based on their own experience.
OppositionalAudience rejects the encoded message and reads it in a completely different or opposite way.

Ways meaning gets encoded: word choice / language in headlines, and under-reporting / gatekeeping (deciding which stories get covered at all).

Media messages aren't "one size fits all". Different audiences decode them differently, and media can both reflect and reinforce stereotypes.

VIP #4 · Noam Chomsky: Manufacturing Consent

MIT professor, historian, social critic and activist; co-author of Manufacturing Consent (with Edward S. Herman).

  • In a democracy, control can't be imposed by force, so limits are placed on democracy through subtle means: the media.
  • Core idea: the media doesn't inform citizens impartially. It manufactures consent for the interests of powerful elites (corporations, government, influential groups).
  • The Propaganda Model: media can work as a propaganda system that looks independent and objective but advances the agendas of the powerful, especially during military conflict.

Our own personal biases

  • Bias: a tendency to believe some people, ideas, etc. are better than others, often leading to unfair treatment.
  • Explicit bias: attitudes and beliefs we hold consciously and deliberately express.
  • Implicit bias: attitudes that operate outside our conscious awareness. Everyone has them, even judges and journalists, but they can be worked on and changed. Explicit and implicit biases can contradict each other.
  • Confirmation bias: favouring information that confirms what we already believe and discounting information that disagrees.
  • Availability bias: thinking examples that come easily to mind are more common or representative than they really are.
  • Negativity bias: negative things affect us more than neutral or positive things of equal intensity.

Documentary study: Control Room, about Al Jazeera's and U.S. media's very different coverage of the 2003 Iraq War, a real case of encoding, sourcing and perspective.

Causes of conflict

First question to ask: is it a war of choice (a state chooses to fight when it doesn't have to) or a war of necessity (fought because a state feels it has no alternative, e.g., self-defence)?

Power & securityCountries seek security, power and influence.
Economic interests & resourcesOil and natural gas, minerals, water, fertile land.
Identity, nationalism & religionEthnic differences, religious beliefs, language, national identity.
Environmental & demographic pressuresRapid population growth strains jobs, infrastructure and political systems.
Political systems & leadershipAuthoritarian regimes may use conflict to distract from domestic problems, use nationalism to build unity, or suppress dissent through external confrontation.
Historical grievances & revengeColonial-era borders, wars that ended without reconciliation, generations raised with collective memories of injustice.
Wars rarely have just one cause. Most have multiple, layered causes.

Security dilemma: when actions a state takes to increase its own security cause reactions from other states, which end up decreasing the original state's security. (Example: NATO expansion feels defensive to members but threatening to Russia; arms races.)

Case study: Russian invasion of Ukraine (power & security)

"Getting inside Putin's head demands the skills not only of a psychologist and historian but of a geographer… it is maps, not history books, that keep him up at night." (Tim Marshall, Prisoners of Geography)
  • History: the Soviet Union (1922–1991) and the Cold War split of NATO vs the Warsaw Pact. Since 1991, many former Warsaw Pact and Soviet states have joined NATO, so Russia has lost its buffer zone.
  • Geography: the North European Plain is flat with few natural barriers (mountains, etc.), an easy invasion route toward Moscow. Russia has historically wanted buffer territory there.
  • Energy: Russia is a global energy superpower: the 2nd-largest oil exporter and holder of the largest natural gas reserves in the world. Pipelines like Nord Stream gave it leverage over Europe.
  • Ukraine as "Europe's next petrostate"? Ukraine has the 14th-largest gas reserves (much in the Black Sea / near Crimea). It lacked the money and technology to develop them, but with Western partners it could become Europe's 2nd-largest petroleum exporter, threatening Russia's wealth and power and making NATO membership more possible.
  • Politics: Yanukovych (2010–2014) rejected an EU deal and aligned with Russia → mass protests → Poroshenko (2014–2019) steered Ukraine toward the EU. Putin responded by annexing Crimea in 2014.

A layered conflict

LayerExplanation
Core: Power & SecurityNATO expansion + loss of buffer zone → Russia fears strategic vulnerability.
Amplified by GeographyFlat North European Plain = little natural defence.
Amplified by IdentityUkraine turning toward Europe and away from Russian influence.
Amplified by Energy & EconomicsBlack Sea oil and gas = wealth, leverage and power in Europe.

Case study: Israel–Palestine (identity)

What happens when two groups both see the same land as central to who they are?

Israeli national identityJewish self-determination after centuries of persecution, culminating in the Holocaust.
Palestinian national identityIndigenous national identity tied to the land, displacement in 1948, and statelessness.

Timeline

  • Late 1800s–early 1900s: increased Jewish immigration due to Zionism, a movement to establish a Jewish homeland. Critics (especially Arab/Palestinian perspectives) argue it was a colonialist movement that displaced the Indigenous population.
  • After WWI: the British Mandate for Palestine (League of Nations). Tensions rose between Jewish and Arab communities.
  • 1947: after the Holocaust raised international sympathy, the UN voted to partition Palestine. Palestinians rejected it as unfair: 55% of the land to Jews, who were ~30% of the population.
  • 1948: Israel declares independence → Arab states invade → Israel wins. Hundreds of thousands of Palestinians are displaced or expelled: the Nakba ("catastrophe"). Many become refugees.
  • 1967 Six-Day War: Israel occupies the West Bank (incl. East Jerusalem), Gaza Strip, Sinai Peninsula and Golan Heights. Settlements in the West Bank are considered illegal under international law; restrictions on movement; land confiscation.
  • Palestinian resistance: armed struggle, political activism, nonviolent resistance. PLO = secular, recognized Israel in 1993. Hamas = Islamist, does NOT recognize Israel.

Social identity theory

People get part of their identity and self-esteem from their groups ("I am Jewish / Palestinian / Israeli / part of X nation"). Group identity becomes personal identity.

1. Social categorization: sort people into in-group / out-group→2. Social identification: adopt the group's narratives, values, memory→3. Social comparison: want our group to feel legitimate, secure, morally justified
  • If your group identity includes centuries of persecution, exile, genocide → threat perception becomes heightened.
  • If it includes displacement, occupation, statelessness → injustice becomes central to identity.

Why identity conflicts are harder to resolve

Conflicts over territory, resources or power are about material interests: you can split land, share resources, sign treaties, adjust borders. When conflict is about identity, compromise can feel like losing your culture, betraying your religion, abandoning historical claims or dishonouring past suffering. The conflict becomes existential, not just strategic.

You can negotiate over how much land you control. It is much harder to negotiate over who you are.

Environmental stress & climate change

Thomas Homer-Dixon: with a population over 8 billion and a growing global economy, the world faces growing scarcity of renewable resources like cropland, fresh water and forests. Environmental degradation leads to:

Decreased agricultural production→Economic decline→Population displacement→Weakened institutions & ethnic tension→Conflict
"…mass migrations of people into a region may disrupt labor markets, shift class relations, and upset the traditional balance of economic and political authority between ethnic groups."
  • Pressure points: migration (e.g., Mediterranean migrant crossings), most of the world's largest cities sit on coasts (sea-level rise), the Arctic opening up (Russia and China pushing claims), and the water–food–energy nexus (rising demand for water ~40%, food ~35%, energy ~50%).
  • Threat multiplier: climate change doesn't directly cause war, but it makes existing problems worse (poverty, weak government, ethnic tension), pushing fragile places over the edge.

Syria

1970s–1990s farming policies drained groundwater → severe drought (about 2006–2010), winter 2007–08 the driest on record → crop failures, food prices doubled, ~1.5 million rural people moved to cities alongside Iraqi refugees → unemployment, anger at the Assad regime → uprising in March 2011.

"We're not saying drought caused the war. We're saying that added to all the other stressors, it helped kick things over the threshold into open conflict." (Prof. Richard Seager, Columbia University)

Why has large-scale war become less common?

Data shows battle deaths dropped after WWII, and the share of years in which the Great Powers fought each other fell from nearly 100% (1500s–1600s) to almost zero since 1953. Reasons covered:

  • Economic interdependence: global trade has exploded, so war is too costly when your enemy is also your customer and supplier.
  • Spread of democracy: far more people live in democracies now, and democracies rarely fight each other (democratic peace theory).
  • Nuclear deterrence: mutually assured destruction makes Great Power war suicidal.
  • International institutions & globalization: the UN, trade bodies and global communication create ways to solve disputes peacefully.
  • Rising prosperity: GDP per capita has grown massively, and wealthy societies have more to lose.

Fukuyama's "End of History"

  • Francis Fukuyama argued that after the Cold War, liberal democracy had emerged as the final form of government. "History," as the clash of grand ideologies, was over.
  • Monarchy, fascism and communism had been defeated, ideologically and practically; democracy, free markets and human rights were spreading.
  • Challenge: is this the end of the "End of History"? Rising authoritarianism: China (Xi Jinping), Russia (Putin), Turkey (Erdoğan), Hungary (Orbán), Venezuela (Maduro). Conflicts this unit (Ukraine, Gaza, Syria) also challenge his idea.

Tools of power: soft vs hard

Soft power (attract & persuade)
  • Co-option: potential opponents become closely tied (e.g., Germany and France).
  • Golden Arches Theory: two countries with McDonald's don't go to war with each other (trade links).
  • Dell Theory: countries in the same global supply chain won't fight each other.
  • Public diplomacy: building ongoing positive relationships with foreign publics.
  • Government diplomacy: international negotiations.
Hard power (coerce)
  • Aid payments & bribes: wealthy countries pay poorer ones for support.
  • Sanctions: measures by countries or organizations (e.g., UN) to force a policy change.
  • Force: war, symmetric (similar militaries) or asymmetric (unequal sides, more common now).

The military-industrial complex

"…we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence… by the military-industrial complex." (President Dwight Eisenhower, 1961)

The close connection between government and weapons companies. Critics say it leads to excessive military spending, unnecessary or dangerous weapons, too much corporate influence on policy, and a preference for military over diplomatic solutions.

Terrorism: 9/11 and beyond

There is no generally accepted definition. Common elements: violence or threat of violence, creating an atmosphere of fear, to coerce others into actions they wouldn't otherwise take. Often called a "tool of the weak".

Freedom fighter or terrorist?

  • "One person's terrorist is another person's freedom fighter."
  • In 1988 the U.S. labelled the ANC and Nelson Mandela terrorists. They fought apartheid; Mandela later became president and won the Nobel Peace Prize.
  • Osama bin Laden fought the Soviets in Afghanistan in the 1980s and was supported by Western governments as a "good guy".
  • Media framing: critics note the word "terrorism" is applied mostly to non-state and Muslim attackers (Jan. 6 Capitol riot: was it domestic terrorism?).

What motivates terrorism?

  • Cultural fanaticism: resisting secularism, believing they are morally right (Taliban, Hezbollah, Timothy McVeigh; Unabomber Ted Kaczynski against industrial society).
  • Psychology: personal dissatisfaction, "us vs. them" (Toronto van attack; European recruits).
  • Resistance to imperialism (real or perceived): IRA in Ireland; Al-Qaeda vs U.S. involvement in the Middle East.
  • Injustice: feeling wronged. Frustration-Aggression Theory: blocked goals → frustration → aggression.

Why did 9/11 happen?

  • Long history of European colonialism; in 1918 the Middle East came under European control; Britain, France and Italy drew borders that caused culture shock and instability.
  • Post-colonial military dictators failed to run economies; the Arab world became the 2nd-poorest region → revolutionary movements.
  • Islamism: the political belief that countries should be reorganized to fully follow Islamic law. Jihadists are Muslims who fight on behalf of the faith; secular means not bound by religious rule.
  • Bin Laden believed Western ideals were inappropriate for Muslims. His answer was Islamism. U.S. troops protecting Saudi Arabia in the 1991 Gulf War infuriated him → founded Al-Qaeda, targeting the U.S.

Related lens: some conflicts are driven by historical grievance, humiliation and revenge.

Game theory

Game theory studies how people, countries or companies make decisions when their choices affect each other. The outcome for one player depends on what others do too.

Rational actors (states, organizations, individuals) consistently act to maximize their own self-interest and anticipate others' responses.

The Prisoner's Dilemma (Liv & Charlie)

Years in prison (Liv, Charlie)Charlie testifiesCharlie stays silent
Liv testifies5, 50, 10
Liv stays silent10, 01, 1
  • Two choices: cooperate (stay silent) or defect (testify).
  • Defection is rational for each individual: whatever the other does, you're better off testifying (0 instead of 1, or 5 instead of 10). You also protect yourself from being betrayed.
  • But if both defect, both get 5 years. Mutual cooperation (1, 1) is better overall.
  • It's a social dilemma: individual interest conflicts with collective well-being. A form of noncooperative game theory.

Cold War applications

  • Arms race: "to arm or not to arm". Both sides arming (defecting) is the PD outcome; nuclear warheads peaked in the mid-1980s.
  • Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): U.S. choices = air strike or lift blockade; USSR = keep or withdraw missiles. Outcomes: U.S. victory, Soviet victory, compromise, or MAD. Not really a PD, because they changed the rules: they communicated. PDs work because players can't talk.
  • Hobbesian trap: preemptive strikes happen out of mutual fear of an imminent attack. Avoided by building trust (e.g., Cuban Missile Crisis communication). MAD = Mutually Assured Destruction.

Zero-sum vs non-zero-sum

Zero-sumOne side's gain = the other's loss. E.g., trade wars, arms races, territorial disputes.
Non-zero-sumAll can win (or all can lose). E.g., trade agreements, climate treaties, arms control treaties (NPT).

Climate change as a PD: every nation benefits if others cut pollution, but each prefers not to cut its own. There are no present-day consequences for defecting, and acting alone can be "political suicide".

The evolution of cooperation

  • Robert Axelrod, The Evolution of Cooperation: in an iterated (repeated) PD, players meet again, so the "shadow of the future" makes cooperation pay. Reputation and retaliation matter.
  • Tit-for-tat (cooperate first, then copy what the other did) won Axelrod's tournaments. Ch. 4 example: "live and let live" truces between enemy soldiers in WWI trenches.
  • Activity: The Evolution of Trust (Nicky Case).

The nuclear weapons debate

Nuclear weapons increase stability
  • Deterrence / MAD: no rational leader starts a war that guarantees their own destruction.
  • No direct war between nuclear powers since 1945; the Cold War stayed "cold".
  • Fits realism: in anarchy, states need the ultimate self-help tool for survival.
  • Forces caution and communication in crises (Cuban Missile Crisis).
Nuclear weapons are dangerous
  • Accidents, false alarms, miscalculation: one mistake = catastrophe.
  • Assumes leaders are always rational; not guaranteed.
  • Proliferation to more states raises risk; possible terrorist access.
  • Security dilemma & arms races (Cold War warheads peaked in the 1980s); huge cost (military-industrial complex).
  • Hobbesian trap: fear can trigger preemptive strikes.

Arms control like the NPT (Non-Proliferation Treaty) is a non-zero-sum attempt to escape the arms-race prisoner's dilemma.

International relations theory: realism vs liberalism

Realism: "the world as it is"

  • Conflict is inevitable; politics is shaped by competition, not cooperation.
  • Nation-states are the main actors, acting as unitary, rational actors in their national interest.
  • The system is in anarchy: no world government or "world police", so it's a self-help system.
  • Humans are selfish, power-hungry and mistrustful → arms races, alliances and war.
  • Balance of power: if one state gets too strong, others band together to check it (e.g., against Nazi Germany).
  • The highest goal is survival of the state. Actions are judged by the ethics of responsibility, not moral principles. War can be a necessary tool of statecraft.
  • Critiques: a self-fulfilling prophecy (assuming distrust causes distrust), too pessimistic, treats the state as one solid unit and ignores other actors and non-survival issues.
  • Example: China's Belt & Road Initiative, using economic investment to extend power and influence.

Liberalism: "cooperation over conflict"

  • Governments should protect individual rights (life, liberty, property); power should be limited and accountable.
  • War is not inevitable; cooperation is possible. Militarism is dangerous.
  • International institutions (UN, NATO, WTO) help build trust, monitor agreements and solve problems peacefully. The more states cooperate, the less likely they are to defect.
  • Interdependence brings peace and prosperity.
  • Many actors matter: states, multinational corporations (MNCs), international organizations, even individuals.
  • Democratic peace theory: democracies rarely or never go to war with each other.

The United Nations: success or struggle?

"Should countries have to follow global rules, even if it goes against their national interests?"

  • Created in 1945 after WWII: 51 members → now 193.
  • UN Charter goals: maintain international peace and security, promote human rights, support economic and social development, uphold international law, provide humanitarian aid.
OrganWhat it is
General AssemblyAll 193 members, 1 vote each
Security Council15 members; 5 permanent with veto (P5: U.S., U.K., France, Russia, China)
SecretariatDay-to-day operations, led by the Secretary-General
ICJ, WHO, UNESCOCourt and specialized agencies

The veto problem: any P5 member can block Security Council action (e.g., vetoes on Syria, Ukraine, Israel–Palestine). Critics say it's undemocratic and protects major powers, showing tension between power and equality in global governance. Realists would say this proves institutions reflect power; liberals argue the UN still matters.
